Convolutions, explained

Interactive pieces for the convolution lecture and LeCun et al. 1989: first, why comparing images pixel by pixel fails as soon as something moves, and the oriented detector Hubel and Wiesel found in the cat’s visual cortex; then the operation itself, in 1D and 2D; why sharing weights makes it shift-equivariant, and pooling makes it invariant; how far each unit sees as layers stack, and where padding creeps in; and the four axes of a layer’s weights.

  1. The problem

    Shift breaks pixel distance

    A digit, the same digit moved a few pixels, and a different digit. Drag the copy and watch its distance from the original climb past the distance to other digits, often after a single pixel, until a nearest-neighbour classifier on pixels calls it something else. Blurring first only postpones it.

  2. Hubel and Wiesel

    Orientation tuning

    Turn a bar of light in front of one detector, the lecture’s −½ +1 −½ edge-finder rotated, and trace its tuning curve while it crackles. Move the bar off centre and it goes silent, which is why the same detector has to be copied to every position; a bank of eight angles reads out the orientation.

  3. The operation

    1D convolution, step by step

    The lecture’s example animated: the three-wide edge-finder slides along a ten-long signal, multiplies, adds, and drops one output per position, with both drawn as stems. Edit any number, try blur or shift, and flip the kernel to see true convolution against the cross-correlation that libraries actually compute.

  4. The operation, in 2D

    2D convolution and oriented edges

    Step a 3×3 window over the lecture’s striped image: the vertical edge-finder lights up and the horizontal one finds nothing. Then draw a picture and convolve it live with edge-finders at any angle, Sobel, blur and sharpen, or weights you drag yourself.

  5. Weight sharing, in action

    Equivariance and invariance

    Shift then convolve, or convolve then shift: the two paths meet exactly, because every position uses the same weights. Pool over positions and the result stops caring where the digit is. Then turn on stride 2 and watch an odd shift break both.

  6. Depth, stride, padding

    Receptive field through the layers

    A 1D network drawn unit by unit: point at any unit to light the cone of inputs it depends on. Stack layers and the field grows by (K−1)·d each; add stride and the growth multiplies. With same padding, watch the band of units that have read zeros widen with depth until edge units near the top see mostly padding.

  7. Four axes

    The 4D weight tensor

    A layer’s weights drawn as a grid of kernels, one row per output channel and one column per input channel. Point at any output pixel to see the patch it reads in every channel, one dot product per kernel, and the bias adding up; switch between C maps and a grid of C-vectors, where a 1×1 convolution is a matrix at every pixel.
