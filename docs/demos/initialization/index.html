Initialization, explained

Four interactive pieces on Glorot & Bengio 2010: why a deep network's signal can vanish or blow up depending on nothing but the scale of its weights, why that same scale controls how much of the backward gradient survives saturation, why forward and backward stability pull the initialization variance in different directions when a layer isn't square, and — instead of just the theory — what the failure actually looks like while a real network trains.

  1. The mechanism

    Depth, activation, and initialization

    Watch a signal spread through a network of adjustable depth — the core empirical result the paper opens with, color-coded against the same saturated/responsive split as the demo below, so you can watch the saturated fraction compound layer over layer.

  2. Why bad scale hurts backprop

    Weight scale and saturation

    1000 units sampled at a chosen weight scale, scattered along tanh or sigmoid with the local derivative shaded beneath. Dial the scale up and watch units pile into the flat tails, where the forward signal survives but the backward gradient is nearly zero.

  3. Where 2/(n_in+n_out) comes from

    Forward vs. backward: Glorot's compromise

    Forward stability wants Var(W)=1/n_in; backward stability wants Var(W)=1/n_out. Set an asymmetric layer and watch neither forward-only nor backward-only Xavier satisfy both — and see why Glorot's averaged formula is the compromise that bounds the damage either way.

  4. The failure, in practice

    Bad init, watched training

    Not just the theory — a real 6-layer tanh network training live on two moons with plain gradient descent. Too-small init and the loss barely moves; too-big init and units saturate immediately and the boundary stays near its random start; Xavier trains cleanly. Decision boundary, loss curve, and layer-wise activation std, all updating together.
