Initialization, explained

Nine interactive pieces on Glorot & Bengio 2010: first, what saturation is on a single tanh; then what the failure looks like while a real network trains, and the chain rule behind it, one neuron per layer, and how the choice of activation changes it; then what variance is, where the n in n·Var(W) comes from (forward and backward), where the 1/3 comes from, the two uniform initializations the paper compares, and why forward and backward stability pull the variance in different directions when a layer isn't square.

  1. Saturation

    Saturation on one tanh

    Twenty units on a single tanh curve, each drawn with its tangent line. Scale their inputs up and the tangents lie flat, so almost no gradient gets back; scale them down and every slope is 1, but the outputs huddle at 0.

  2. The failure, in practice

    Bad init, watched training

    Not just the theory — a real 6-layer tanh network training live on two moons with plain gradient descent. Too-small init and the loss barely moves; too-big init and units saturate immediately and the boundary stays near its random start; Xavier trains cleanly. Decision boundary, loss curve, and per-layer activation histograms (or the whole network drawn neuron by neuron, weight by weight), all updating together.

  3. Why gradients vanish

    Chain rule, one neuron deep

    Five tanh layers with one neuron each, drawn as a forward/backward computation graph. The gradient that reaches each weight is a product you can read off the graph, and it collapses for one of two reasons: a flat slope where a unit is saturated, or a near-zero input at a × node. Turn the weights up or down and watch which one bites.

  4. tanh, sigmoid, relu

    Activations, watched training

    The live training demo with a switch between tanh, sigmoid and relu, and a He init button. Sigmoid stalls at Xavier because its slope is at most 1/4 and only trains with several times larger weights; relu is linear or off, loses half its variance per layer under Xavier, and holds steady under He.

    Variance: an average square, and four rules

    Variance drawn as the average of squares hung on the samples, and the four rules that make it easy to push through a layer: scaling, sums, products, and their consequence Var(h) = n·Var(W)·Var(x).

  6. Where the n comes from

    A matrix product of random numbers

    A matrix-vector product animated with every entry drawn as its probability density. Forward, each output adds up n products, so its variance is n·σw²; backward, the gradient goes through WT and each input's gradient adds up m. Glorot's dilemma, then several layers stacked.

  7. Where the 1/3 comes from

    Why a uniform has variance a²/3

    Variance is an average square. Stack a uniform's squares and they form a pyramid in an a×a×a box; three such pyramids fill the box, so the average square is a third of the biggest.

    Standard vs. normalized init

    The paper's two uniform initializations side by side, as histograms and scatterplots, with the variance of a uniform on [−a, a] derived two ways: as an integral, and as the mean height of a parabola, which fills 1/3 of its bounding box.

  9. Where 2/(n_in+n_out) comes from

    Forward vs. backward: Glorot's compromise

    Forward stability wants Var(W)=1/n_in; backward stability wants Var(W)=1/n_out. Set an asymmetric layer and watch neither forward-only nor backward-only Xavier satisfy both — and see why Glorot's averaged formula is the compromise that bounds the damage either way.
