Units vs. random directions: the paper’s first property, as a guessing game

People inspect a network by finding the inputs that most excite one neuron, and then naming what those inputs share. Szegedy et al. ran the same search for a random direction in the same layer, a random weighted mix of all the neurons, and found the results just as coherent. What the paper says. Write φ(x) for the activations of a layer. The usual analysis takes a single unit, the natural basis vector ei, and collects the held-out images x that maximize ⟨φ(x), ei⟩, the unit’s activation. The paper takes a random vector v instead and maximizes ⟨φ(x), v⟩. For both an MNIST network and AlexNet, the two kinds of image set looked equally semantically coherent (their Figures 1–4). So, they conclude, “the natural basis is not better than a random basis,” and it is the whole space of activations, not individual units, that carries the meaning. This is the property Joseph and Ling asked about. Here the network is the paper’s fully connected FC100-100-10, the images are 1,000 held-out test digits, and each random direction is a Gaussian vector over the 100 units of the layer.
Why it matters, and what came after. If meaning lives in directions rather than neurons, then looking at single neurons can miss most of what a network represents, and a neuron that looks like a “7 detector” might be one view of something spread across many units. Later interpretability work took both sides: some units really are selective (network dissection found many single-concept units in convolutional networks), but many representations are distributed and superposed, which is why methods now look for directions (probes, sparse dictionaries) as much as for neurons. Ling’s question, whether there is a way to explain every feature when features are not single neurons, is close to the current research frontier.
