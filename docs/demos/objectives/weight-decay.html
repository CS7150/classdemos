4. Weight decay inside Adam: L2 is not decay (AdamW)

For plain gradient descent, adding λ/2·‖θ‖² to the loss and shrinking the weights every step are the same thing (part 1). Inside Adam they are not, because Adam divides each weight’s step by the size of that weight’s own recent gradients, and whatever you put into the gradient gets divided too, including the penalty.

Adam with an L2 penalty (torch.optim.Adam, weight_decay=λ)
AdamW: decoupled weight decay (torch.optim.AdamW)

The only difference is where λθ goes. In Adam with L2 it is added to the gradient, so it passes through m and v and gets divided by √v̂, which is roughly the size of that weight’s gradients. In AdamW it is added after the division, so every weight loses the same fraction per step:

A weight with large gradients (big √v̂) barely feels the Adam-with-L2 decay. A weight with tiny gradients has its decay divided by a tiny number, and when its gradients are smaller than λθ the decay dominates v itself, so the weight moves toward 0 at Adam’s full step size η per step. Below, eight weights all start at 1 with gradients of different sizes that flip sign every step and average to zero, so the decay is the only force that moves them.

Why it matters. Weight decay is meant to be a prior: pull every weight toward 0 by the same fraction, so the ones the data doesn’t need fade away. Inside Adam, the L2 version does nearly the opposite. The weights with big, frequent gradients, often the ones doing the most work, are hardly regularized at all; weights with small or rare gradients, such as the embedding of a rare word, are wiped out at full speed whether the data needs them or not. Loshchilov and Hutter (“Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization,” ICLR 2019) proposed taking the decay out of the gradient, and found that it makes Adam generalize better and makes good values of λ much less tangled up with the learning rate. AdamW is now the default optimizer for training transformers. In SGD the two versions differ only by a rescaling of λ (part 1), which is why the difference went unnoticed until adaptive optimizers. (In the paper the whole update is also multiplied by a schedule factor ηt; PyTorch’s AdamW does the same thing by first shrinking θ ← (1 − ηλ)θ and then taking the Adam step, with a default λ of 0.01.)

5. Strong decay turns max margin into mean difference

Train a linear classifier, logistic regression with weight decay λ, on two separable classes. With no decay its direction heads for the max-margin separator, the one that stays as far as possible from the closest points. With strong decay it heads somewhere quite different: the mean difference μ+ − μ−, the direction of the old nearest-mean (“Euclidean” or minimum-distance) classifier, which ignores the individual points and looks only at the two class averages. The data here is built so those two directions disagree.

weight decay λ 0.001

Why the mean difference. Write the logistic loss in terms of each point’s margin mi = yi(w·xi + b), with yi = ±1. Strong decay keeps w tiny, so every margin is near 0, where the loss is nearly a straight line:

Keeping only the straight-line part, the objective is linear in w plus the decay, and its minimum is a scaled copy of the average of yixi, which for balanced classes is the mean difference:

Every point pulls on w equally, however far it is from the boundary, so only the averages survive. Why max margin. With little decay on separable data, w can grow without bound, and as it grows the loss e−m of all but the closest points becomes negligible; only the points nearest the boundary still pull, and w/‖w‖ approaches the max-margin direction (Rosset, Zhu & Hastie, 2004), the same direction plain gradient descent drifts toward with no decay at all (Soudry et al., 2018). In between, the next term of the expansion gives roughly w ≈ (λI + ¼ΣxxT/n)−1·½ mean(yx): a ridge-regularized version of Fisher’s discriminant, the Mahalanobis cousin of the nearest-mean rule. So the decay dial slides the classifier from “trust the closest points” to “trust only the averages.”
