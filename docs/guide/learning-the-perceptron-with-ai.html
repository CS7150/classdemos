Using an AI agent to learn the perceptron algorithm — an annotated conversation

How can you steer an AI to teach you something new that you don’t know yet? AI agents are trained to follow your instructions, and they can be great teachers, but it can be a challenge to get them to teach you something you haven’t learned yet. Here is an annotated summary of a conversation you can try yourself, showing several techniques for doing it.

The conversation is about learning Rosenblatt’s perceptron algorithm, and how to prove that it converges. Even though the AI can give a proof in a single prompt, it took a few hours of conversation to get the AI to really explain it well. Along the way I am asking it to make a pretty visual explanation as an interactive JavaScript widget.

The centre column has the prompts, what came back, and the figure as it stood at that moment. The margin has what I was thinking that led to the next question.

Three challenges to overcome when learning from an AI agent

You do not understand it yet. That is the situation you are trying to get out of, so you cannot specify what you want up front. You can only react to what you are shown.

The agent will build what you asked for. Including when what you asked for is wrong. It is much better at satisfying a request than at telling you the request was malformed.

You can both be wrong at once. The failure that matters is not the agent inventing a fact. It is the two of you settling on a picture that looks right, reads well, and quietly misrepresents the theorem. Nothing in the conversation will flag this. Only a check will.

The transcript below shows ways to overcome all three of these problems.

Part one — getting the first picture

This half ran in an ordinary chat. Interactive artifacts are not kept in shared conversations, so two of the figures here are redrawn from screenshots; the rest were rebuilt by running the original code for each step.

You“I’m going to teach Rosenblatt’s algorithm today. What is the best way to teach it? Remind me exactly the algorithm.”

The algorithm came back in four lines. Augment each x with a constant 1 so the bias rides along in w, take labels y ∈ {+1, −1}, and:

w ← 0
repeat until no mistakes:
    for each (x, y) in the training set:
        if y (w · x) ≤ 0:        # a mistake, ties included
            w ← w + y x

Then Novikoff’s bound. If some unit w* separates the data with margin γ = mini yi(w*·xi) > 0, and every ∥xi∥ ≤ R, then the algorithm makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes, whatever order the examples arrive in.

And the proof, in three steps:

  1. Every mistake grows the projection: w*·wk ≥ w*·wk−1 + γ, so w*·wk ≥ kγ.
  2. Every mistake barely grows the length: ∥wk∥² = ∥wk−1∥² + 2y(wk−1·x) + ∥x∥² ≤ ∥wk−1∥² + R², because the middle term is ≤ 0 exactly when it was a mistake. So ∥wk∥ ≤ √k·R.
  3. Cauchy–Schwarz: kγ ≤ w*·wk ≤ ∥wk∥ ≤ √k·R, which rearranges to k ≤ (R/γ)².

Correct, complete, and delivered in one prompt — but too compact for any intuition.

This is a fine proof, and I didn't understand it

That proof is all fine and good if you are a mathematician who is very familiar with making Cauchy–Schwarz inequality arguments. For everybody else it lacks intuition. Nothing in it is wrong; it just doesn't tell you why any of it is true.

If you don't understand something an AI agent has told you, the most important thing to do is to say so. I did that in about twenty different ways over the rest of this conversation, until we ended up with something I actually understood.

Say “I don't understand” out loud, and keep saying it. A correct answer you can't follow is not the end of the exchange; it is the start of one.

You“Simplify the proof for me; make it common-sense for whiteboard argument.”

“Whiteboard version: two gauges on the board, one counting real progress, one counting bloat. Progress climbs steadily. Bloat can't keep up. When they cross, you're out of mistakes.”

Then the units, set first so the rest reads in one scale: the longest input has length 1, and γ is how far the good separator keeps points off the fence. Gauge 1 is the shadow on true north, climbing by at least γ a mistake. Gauge 2 is the length, which cannot keep up because the correction always leans backward.

That vocabulary — gauges, true north, progress against bloat — is the agent’s own jargon. It thinks it is crystal clear, and it cannot tell that it is meaningless to a person.

I asked for plain words before I asked for a picture

When I asked for a whiteboard proof, it didn't summarise the algebra. It gave me words I might actually say out loud at a board: two gauges, one counting progress and one counting bloat, and the moment they cross.

The words sketched the intuition, but so briefly that on their own they were almost nonsensical. That is a common AI problem. No worries though — I was after a figure.

Ask for the common-sense version early, and separately from asking for a drawing. They are different questions, and the answer to one is not a specification for the other.

You“Please draw the diagram here for me. Maybe with js/svg?”

What came back was a plot of the two sides of the inequality: kγ rising as a straight line, √k as a curve, a slider for γ, and the crossing point running away as the margin shrinks.

Then a second figure, for why the length barely grows: the correction leans backward against w at more than 90°, so the triangle closes short of the Pythagorean case.

You“The plot is not informative, but I think that the triangles could be. But you’ve plotted w without plotting w* and you are not showing the mass of other points. I think to explain it would be helpful to show the points.”

“Fair.” Redrawn with the data, the true separator, its margin corridor, and w’s boundary rotating as you step through mistakes.

I didn't know what I wanted yet

When I asked for a figure, I didn't really know what I wanted. So the agent literally drew a plot of two numbers from the proof, which doesn't visually relate to anything about the original problem at all.

It was a bad start, and I realised I would need to be much more specific about what I wanted to see. The triangle it drew next felt closer at the time, but looking back neither figure was informative: both are pictures of the algebra, with no data, no w and no w* anywhere in them.

Check whether the figure contains the things the theorem is about, or only quantities computed from them. If the data isn't in it, say which parts of the setup you want drawn.

You“I like the margin figure, but the ringed point you’ve drawn is not on the wrong side. We need some examples that are on the wrong side!”

“Caught it: I was ringing the point and applying the update in the same click, so you only ever saw the boundary that had already fixed it.” Split into two clicks — find the violation, look at it, then apply.

Running that build also shows the second defect, the one that surfaces in the next exchange: after a single update 0 of its 14 points are misclassified, so a second click has nothing to find.

Even though they are great at code, agents make plenty of bugs.

The picture contradicted its own label

The agent told me the ringed point was a mistake. I looked at it, and it was sitting on the correct side of the line. Once you actually look, that isn't subtle.

The ring and the update were happening on the same click, so I was only ever seeing the boundary after it had already been fixed. Splitting that into two clicks is why the finished demo has a Find mistake step at all.

When a figure claims something about a specific element, find that element and check the claim by eye. This kind of bug doesn't show up in the code that computes the answer.

You“I liked the previous visualization better. Except that find a mistake didn’t work! I want a 2d scatterplot of data points where the initial model misclassifies many of them.”

“The dataset was the problem: after one update w already separated everything, so the second click had nothing to find.” Rebuilt starting from a badly wrong w, 12 of 16 points misclassified at the outset, every wrong point carrying a red halo.

Agents pick easy examples. An easy example makes the demo run, which is not the same as making it show anything.

The reply also said the halo count “does not fall monotonically” and that somewhere in the middle a correction fixes one point and breaks two. Running the whole sequence, the count goes 12, 12, 12, 6, 2, 0 and never rises once. True of the algorithm, not true of this figure.

Nothing was broken, and nothing was being shown

I clicked Find a mistake and it didn't work. Not a crash — there simply wasn't a mistake left to find, because after one update the data was already separated.

So the figure was correct and demonstrating nothing. I asked for data where the model starts out getting most of the points wrong, which is the 4/16 default the demo is still built around.

Something I didn't catch at the time: the agent told me the error count doesn't fall monotonically, and that somewhere a correction fixes one point and breaks two. Running the whole sequence, it goes 12, 12, 12, 6, 2, 0 and never rises once. True of the algorithm, not of that figure.

Ask what the hardest honest case looks like and make it the default. A demo that succeeds immediately has nothing left to show.

You“It’s great, except that it doesn’t show how w is updated from the error case. Is it a parallelogram?”

Yes, and the second side is the data point itself: the correction y·x is the point’s own position vector drawn from the origin, flipped through the origin when the label is negative. So w and the point are two sides and the new w is the diagonal.

Which makes visible something the pseudocode hides: the correction has no free parameters. No gradient, no step size, no loss being differentiated.

I guessed, and said the guess out loud

I could half-see the shape of the update, so I asked whether it was a parallelogram rather than asking how the update should be drawn.

Naming the guess got me a sharper answer: yes, and the second side is the data point itself, which means the correction has no free parameters at all. No gradient, no step size, nothing to tune.

When you half-see a structure, put it in the question. A specific guess gets a better answer than an open request.

You“You haven’t highlighted the narrowing solution space, but I’d like that.”

Added as a violet wedge that starts unbounded and is sliced by each constraint the run bumps into, closing onto the green solution wedge. Presented alongside the convergence discussion, with several paragraphs on what it shows.

You“The cone on the right that you said is used to prove convergence doesn’t really make sense to me — e.g. the w isn’t in that blueish cone — what’s it bounding? Are we drawing the wrong vector?”

“You’re right, and the fault is mine: that violet wedge bounds the solutions, not w. w is never promised to be inside it. It’s bookkeeping about which constraints have been ruled out, and it has no role in the convergence proof. I put it next to the proof talk and made it look load-bearing.”

The region that does bound w is a different one: centred on w*, half-angle arccos(√k·γ/R).

Objecting got me more than a correction. It got me the right object, and the finished figure is built on it.

I asked for it, got it, and then it made no sense

I asked for the narrowing solution space to be drawn, and it was — a violet wedge closing in as the run went on. Then the agent explained it to me as the convergence argument, at length.

But when I looked, w wasn't inside it. So I asked the simplest question I had: what is this thing bounding, and are we drawing the wrong vector?

The wedge bounds the solutions, not w, and has no role in the proof at all. It was correct geometry sitting next to the proof and borrowing its authority. The region that does bound w is a different one, and it is the ancestor of the pink region in the finished demo.

For each region in a figure, name what it constrains and check that thing is where it should be. “Nothing, it's context” is a perfectly good answer — what matters is not letting a region borrow the proof's authority by sitting next to it.

You“On the current data shown, why is it that after the second iteration the w vector is not in the blue cone? Is the blue cone more aggressive than the real bound?”

“Good catch, and the answer is yes.” Both gauges assume w starts at zero. With a nonzero start they pick up offsets, so the honest bound is cosθk ≥ (w*·w₀ + kγ) / √(|w₀|² + kR²). The default start pointed away from w*, making the numerator negative for the first few steps, where the true region is the whole plane.

You“Certainly when k=0 the blue cone should be the full 360, right?”

Right: with no mistakes yet, nothing has been paid off and the bound constrains nothing. The slider had not gone down to 0, and the vacuous case is now drawn as a full disc.

The example was too easy again

The cone was right, but the run ended almost immediately, so the squeeze the proof is about never appeared on screen.

I asked for something more prototypical. The fix was in the data rather than the code: push two points up against the corridor edges, which drops the margin without changing the radius. The bound went from 7 to 28 and the run got long enough to talk over.

When a demonstration finishes too easily, the knob is usually in the instance, not the code. Ask which quantity in the bound controls the difficulty, and set it deliberately.

You“To explain the bounds cone, I think we should show a hypothetical worst-case rollout of the worst possible zig-zag of updates, starting from the worst case and updating up to step t, showing how it stays in the cone.”

The extremal walk is pinned: every step must add exactly γ of shadow and exactly R² of length-squared, so the tip sits at kγ horizontally and √k·R in radius, riding the cone edge at every k. Three conditions, each the extreme of one inequality in the proof.

With a caveat offered unprompted: staying perpendicular to w at every step needs a fresh direction each time, so this trajectory requires dimension at least k. In two dimensions it dies after a couple of steps, which is why the real runs sit well inside the region rather than on its edge.

You“How hard is it to construct data points and an unlucky ordering that actually exercises the worst case?”

“Trivially easy in high dimensions, and apparently impossible in two. The lever is dimension, not ordering.” Then the construction: the standard basis in Rn, all labelled +1, gives exactly n mistakes against a bound of n.

And an admission: “What the worst case actually is in fixed small d, I don’t know off the top of my head, and I’d want to check rather than guess.”

I wanted to see the bound reached, not just asserted

I asked for a hypothetical worst-case rollout, and its shape turned out to be pinned exactly: every step adds γ of shadow and R² of length, so the tip rides the cone edge at every k.

Then the agent volunteered something I hadn't asked about — that walk needs a fresh perpendicular direction at every step, so it needs dimension at least k and cannot happen in two dimensions at all. And when I asked how to construct the worst case for real, it said plainly that it didn't know the answer in small fixed dimension and would want to check rather than guess.

Ask what the figure promises that the situation can't deliver. An agent willing to tell you a construction is unreachable in the case you're drawing is giving you more than one that explains it well.

You“The blue cone is nice now, but the example you’ve shown has an initialization that is too favourable and doesn’t iterate enough. Give me a more prototypical example with more iterations.”

Margin narrowed to about 0.19 of the data radius, with two large-norm points near the corridor edges that keep knocking w past each other. The bound goes from 7 mistakes to 28, and the run takes a while.

The same question again, and it caught something else

Later on I noticed w outside the blue cone after the second iteration, and asked whether the cone was more aggressive than the real bound.

It was. Both gauges assume w starts at zero, and my default didn't. With a nonzero start the bound picks up offset terms, and for the first few steps it constrains nothing at all.

Then I checked the other end: at k = 0 the cone should be the whole plane. It wasn't, because the slider didn't go down to zero.

Keep one containment check and re-run it after every change. Check the ends too — what should the figure show where the theorem says nothing, and does it?

Part two — rebuilding what it meant

The first conversation ended by handing the page to a coding agent with repository access. From here the figures are recoverable, pulled from the commits themselves.

What part one produced

A working demo of the perceptron mistake bound. It ran, it was correct, and it looked finished. Most of its elements changed meaning over the rounds that follow.

You“When it finds a mistake, don’t take the first one it happens to scan. Sort the mistakes by which produces the worst updated w — the maximum angle with w* — and pick among the worst two.”

Implemented, then measured rather than asserted: over 197 picks the chosen update was the worst available every time. The random choice between two never varied.

Each point sat in the array twice, as +x and as −x labelled minus, and both produce the same update y·x. The two worst were one update counted twice. Deduplicating by update vector made the choice real: 98/68 across the worst and second-worst.

I asked whether it worked; the number said it hadn't

I asked for the worst mistake to be picked, choosing among the worst two. The agent said done.

What actually surfaced the problem was asking for counts. Over 197 picks the chosen update was the worst available every single time, so the random choice between two never varied at all. Each point sat in the array twice, as +x and −x, and both give the same update.

Don't accept “done” for a change in behaviour. Name a count and a number of trials, and read the result before you believe it.

You“Make the positive and negative cases unsymmetric when generating the points. And pick the initial w within 15° of −w*.”

This is where the duplication came from. The generator emitted points in antipodal pairs, so the cloud was point-symmetric and the two halves of each pair shared one update. Drawing each point independently removes it at the source.

Starting w near −w* begins the run in the hardest honest state. Across 20 datasets: initial angle 166°–179°, label splits varying (17/15, 20/12, 14/18), no surviving symmetric pairs, no run over its own bound.

I went after the cause instead of the symptom

Deduplicating fixed the symptom. The cause was upstream: the generator was producing points in antipodal pairs, so the whole cloud was point-symmetric — a special case I had been demonstrating without knowing it.

So I asked for the two classes to be drawn independently, and for w to start within 15° of −w*, which is the hardest honest starting state.

When a check turns up a degeneracy, trace it back to where it was created before accepting the patch.

You“Is worst angle to w* the right heuristic, or should we be sorting by worst dot product?”

Answered with a benchmark: over 40 datasets replayed from identical starts, angle-ordering forced more mistakes (mean 19.3 vs 17.1, winning 27 of 40). The two orders disagreed on 56% of steps.

You“You can reason about it from the structure of the proof.”

The bound constrains a ratio, cosθ = (w*·w)/|w|. Since w*·w is common to every candidate, dot-product ordering reduces to argmin over margins, which tightens the numerator and leaves the denominator slack. Angle ordering targets the quantity being bounded. There is also a sign flip: while the shadow is negative the adversary wants |w| small, and once it is positive, large. Dot-ordering never sees the denominator, so it cannot follow that.

I didn't want the measurement, I wanted the reason

I asked whether angle or dot product was the right heuristic, and got a benchmark: angle forces more mistakes, winning 27 of 40 datasets. That tells me which without telling me why.

So I said: reason about it from the structure of the proof. That answer was the useful one. The bound is on a ratio, and dot-product ordering only tightens the top of it while leaving the bottom slack.

Given a number, ask for the argument. Given an argument, ask for the number. Both were worth having here — the argument explained the ranking, and the measurement showed the two orders disagree often enough for the choice to matter.

You“The worst-case cone should have another colour so we can talk about it out loud in class unambiguously. Yellow or pink?”

Pink. Amber was already w and its separating plane, which sit inside the cone in the same panel, so yellow would have produced a yellow arrow inside a yellow cone.

You“Why does it say cone: every direction?”

That label was written when pink meant only the cone. By this point it announced that nothing was constrained, printed across a visibly bounded region.

Naming it out loud is what caught the stale label

I asked what colour the region should be so I could point at it in class without ambiguity. Pink, because amber was already w and its separating plane, sitting inside the same panel.

A while later I noticed the label still read cone: every direction, printed across a region that was visibly bounded. It had been written two versions earlier and nobody had re-read it.

After each change to the figure, read every word still on it. Trying to name a thing out loud is a cheap way to find out whether you have pinned it down.

You“Would it be informative for the pink cone to also show the bounds for the length of w?”

This one changed the figure most. What the two inequalities pin down is a circular cap: the disc of radius √(|w₀|²+kR²) cut by the chord at w*·w₀+kγ. The cone was its angular hull, which is a projection of that. Drawing the cap shows the mechanism the cone states: the chord moves outward linearly in k while the arc grows as √k, and the cone closes when they meet.

I asked whether the length bounds could be shown too

This is the question that changed the figure most. What the two inequalities pin down isn't a cone at all — it's a disc cut by a chord. The cone was only its angular shadow.

Drawing the real region shows the mechanism instead of stating it: the chord marches outward linearly in k, the arc grows as the square root, and the cone closes at the moment they meet.

Ask what set the theorem actually permits, then insist that set is the thing drawn. A picture of a consequence can be completely correct and still carry less.

You“When showing the worst-case path, maybe it will touch the corner of this arc in a way we can understand?”

It does. The worst-case tip at step j sits at radius rad(j) and angle ±arccos(shad(j)/rad(j)), which are the coordinates of the corner where chord meets arc — checked to 3×10−15 over 615 tips. There are two corners, which is why it alternates.

The check also turned up a defect. wcTip had been using the clamped cone angle, 180° while the shadow is negative, which placed the tip at radius rad(j) in a direction with w*·w = −rad(j) — below the shadow floor. The extremal run was being drawn outside the region the proof allows. Corrected, its step-0 corner is w₀.

I said what I expected before it was checked

I guessed the worst-case path would touch the corner of the arc. It does, exactly — the tip sits on that corner at every step, to fifteen decimal places.

The guess being nearly right is what found the bug. The path had been drawn using the clamped angle, which put it outside the region the proof allows. Corrected, it starts at w₀, where it should.

Say the guess before the check, and ask for a tolerance over many instances so that “yes” carries information.

You“Why wouldn’t the spray be such that all the blue rays go on one side and all the white rays on the other? I guess that won’t happen unless we already apply the separating plane rule to know which direction to draw the ray, right?”

The colours interleave because the ray is y·x, so the label is already folded in; that multiplication is what maps both classes into one fan. Drawing raw x separates them, and needs only the labels.

The premise was close to something else, though: the y·x fan fitting inside a half-plane is linear separability. All 640 rays measured within 84° of w*.

You“Switch to raw x.”

Done, with the cost stated: for a − point the update runs along the mirror ray, so the red arrow lies on its own ray only half the time.

You“Now that you’ve drawn it, I see. Mathematically the right thing in weight space is the dual hyperplanes — the line orthogonal to each point. Let’s go back to the dual lines, coloured by class.”

Back to {w : w·x = 0} per point, full lines, in class colours. That also brings back the wedge’s construction: it is the overlap of the half-planes these bound, and its two edges lie along two of the drawn lines, to 1.2×10−3, which is the step size of the search that locates them.

I built the wrong thing on purpose, and it worked

I expected the blue and the white points to fan out to opposite sides, and they didn't. The explanation was that the ray is y·x, so the label is already folded in.

I asked for raw x anyway, just to see it. And seeing it is what told me the right answer, which was neither of the two on the table: in weight space a point is a line, not a direction. So we went back to the dual hyperplanes.

Three versions to end up near where we started, with me understanding it. The discussion beforehand hadn't got there.

When two designs are hard to choose between and both are cheap to build, build both. And ask what a change makes worse — here the answer decided it later.

The same confusion produced a result. w has to sit within 90° of every ray, so within 90° of both extremes; if those are S apart, what satisfies both is an interval of width 180° − S. So fan span + wedge width = 180°, measured 179.87 to 179.98 across 20 datasets, the residual being the 0.0012 rad step of the search.

A tight fan leaves a wide wedge. A fan spreading toward 180° leaves none, which is separability failing.

The confusion was worth following

My expectation about the colours was simply wrong about the figure as drawn. Chasing it anyway produced a redrawn figure, a correction to the geometry, and a clean identity: the fan's spread plus the wedge's width always comes to 180°.

Ask about things that seem like they ought to be true, even when you suspect the answer is boring. Phrase it as a claim, so there is something specific to refute.

You“When we choose a mistake, should we put a green dot in the right place in weight space? It would show up on the corresponding spray line.”

The agent picked one reading, built a crossing-point marker, measured its distribution over 191 updates, found a step-dependent pattern in it, and wrote it up.

You“Maybe you are imagining a different dot than I am?”

Three candidates existed — the tip of y·x, the tip of w+y·x, and the crossing on the line — and the request did not separate them. Listing all three led to a fourth answer: at that stage a point has no position in weight space, because its dual is a line.

It built the wrong thing, carefully

I asked for a green dot. The agent picked one of several possible meanings, built it, measured its distribution across 191 updates, and wrote the whole thing up. All of it careful, and none of it what I meant.

When I said maybe you're imagining a different dot than I am, the three candidates finally came out — and the real answer was a fourth thing neither of us had said.

For anything ambiguous, ask for the list before the implementation: name the candidates, say which satisfy the constraint, and wait.

You“Maybe label it pink: Cauchy–Schwarz limit, or pink: bounded region for worst-case w?”

Neither fits. The region is the conjunction of the two inductive bounds; Cauchy–Schwarz is the step applied to it to get the cone, so that name belongs to the input. And it holds every w still permitted, not the worst one, which is its corner. The label became “where w must lie after k mistakes” — necessary rather than sufficient.

I proposed two names and both were wrong

I suggested calling the region the Cauchy–Schwarz limit, or the bounded region for the worst case. The agent pushed back on both, and was right to.

Cauchy–Schwarz is the step you apply to the region to get the cone, so that name belongs to the input. And the region holds every w still permitted, not just the worst one, which is only its corner.

Propose names deliberately and ask for arguments against them. A wrong name repeats, and the figure never contradicts it.
