Interrogating a proof with an LLM — an annotated transcript

This is a cleaned-up record of building the perceptron convergence demo in conversation with an LLM agent. The visualization is not the point. The point is the sequence of doubts that produced it, because that sequence is what understanding the proof actually consists of.

Read the centre column for what was said and what appeared on screen. Read the blue column for what the person was thinking in order to arrive at the next question. The blue column is the content; the prompts are only evidence.

Three things working against you

You do not understand it yet. That is the situation you are trying to get out of, so you cannot specify what you want up front. You can only react to what you are shown.

The model will build what you asked for. Including when what you asked for is wrong. It is much better at satisfying a request than at telling you the request was malformed.

You can both be wrong at once. The failure that matters is not the model hallucinating a fact. It is the two of you settling on a picture that looks right, reads well, and quietly misrepresents the theorem. Nothing in the conversation will flag this. Only a check will.

Everything below is a defence against one of those three.

Where it started

A working demo of the perceptron mistake bound. It runs, it is correct, and it looks finished. Nine questions later almost every element of it had changed meaning.

The transcript

You“When it finds a mistake, don’t take the first one it happens to scan. Sort the mistakes by which produces the worst updated w — the maximum angle with w* — and pick among the worst two.”

Implemented. Then, asked to measure it rather than assert it: over 197 picks the chosen update was the worst available every single time — the “random choice between two” never actually varied.

The cause: each point sat in the array twice, as +x and as −x labelled minus, and both produce the identical update y·x. The two worst were one update wearing two hats. Deduplicating by update vector made the choice real: 98/68 across the worst and second-worst.

Ask for the number, not the behaviour

“Did it work?” returns yes. It will almost always return yes.

What found the bug was demanding a distribution: over 200 picks, how often was the choice the worst available, and how many distinct updates existed? A model that has to produce that table cannot paper over a degenerate case.

Concretely: never accept “done” for a behavioural change. Name a count, a tolerance, and a number of trials, and ask for the result before you believe it.

You“Make the positive and negative cases unsymmetric when generating the points. And pick the initial w within 15° of −w*.”

This is the root cause of the duplication above. The generator had been emitting points in antipodal pairs — every x came with a −x labelled oppositely — so the cloud was point-symmetric and the two halves of each pair shared one update. Drawing each point independently removes the degeneracy at its source.

Starting w near −w* makes the run begin in the worst honest state. Verified across 20 datasets: initial angle 166°–179°, label splits varying (17/15, 20/12, 14/18), zero surviving symmetric pairs, no run exceeding its own bound.

Fix the cause, not the symptom

The previous beat found that two candidate updates were secretly identical. The tempting repair is to deduplicate and move on — which was done, and which was not enough.

The real question is why were there duplicates, and the answer was upstream, in how the data was made. A demo that generates symmetric data is quietly demonstrating a special case.

When a check catches a degeneracy, ask where it came from before accepting the patch. Then ask what the hardest honest configuration is, and make that the default — a bound that is never stressed is a bound nobody learns anything from.

You“Is worst angle to w* the right heuristic, or should we be sorting by worst dot product?”

Answered with a benchmark: over 40 datasets replayed from identical starts, angle-ordering forced more mistakes than dot-ordering (mean 19.3 vs 17.1, winning 27 of 40). The two orderings disagreed on 56% of steps.

You“You can reason about it from the structure of the proof.”

The bound constrains a ratio, cosθ = (w*·w)/|w|. Since w*·w is common to every candidate, dot-product ordering collapses to argmin over margins and nothing else — it tightens the numerator and lets the denominator go slack. Angle ordering targets the quantity the theorem actually bounds. There is even a sign flip: while the shadow is negative the adversary wants |w| small, and once positive it wants it large. Dot-ordering cannot see that, because it never looks at the denominator.

A benchmark is not an explanation

The first answer was a measurement. Measurements tell you which without telling you why, and a why is what transfers to the next problem.

Pushing once — “derive it from the proof” — is the highest-yield move in this whole transcript. If the model cannot produce the derivation, that is diagnostic: it has a correlation, not a model, and at that moment neither do you.

Run it both directions. Given a benchmark, ask for the derivation. Given a derivation, ask for the benchmark. Here both were worth having — the derivation explained the ranking, and the measurement showed the two heuristics disagree often enough for the choice to matter.

You“The worst-case cone should have another colour so we can talk about it out loud in class unambiguously. Yellow or pink?”

Pink. Amber is already w and its separating plane, which sit inside the cone in the same panel — “the yellow arrow inside the yellow cone” is exactly the ambiguity being removed.

You“Why does it say cone: every direction?”

Because that label was written when pink meant only the cone. It now announces that nothing is constrained, printed across a visibly bounded region. A fossil from two versions earlier.

Read the caption against the picture

A figure and its label are two separate claims. When they disagree, the label is usually a fossil — and fossils mark precisely where the meaning changed without anyone noticing.

Note what triggered the catch: asking what do I call this out loud in class? Forcing yourself to name a thing is a cheap contradiction detector, because a name that will not survive being said in a room is usually a name for something you have not pinned down.

Every time the visualization changes, re-read every word on it. The stale label is where your own understanding silently moved.

You“Would it be informative for the pink cone to also show the bounds for the length of w?”

This turned out to be the most important question asked. What the two inequalities pin down is not a cone: it is a circular cap, the disc of radius √(|w₀|²+kR²) cut by the chord at w*·w₀+kγ. The cone was only its angular hull — a lossy projection. Drawing the cap shows the mechanism the cone merely asserts: the chord marches outward linearly in k while the arc grows as √k, and when they meet, the cone has closed.

Is the picture the object, or a shadow of it?

The cone was not wrong. It was a consequence of the real constraint, and a picture of a consequence looks perfectly correct while teaching strictly less.

The unlocking question is blunt: what exactly is the set of w the proof still permits after k mistakes? Then insist that set be what gets drawn. Everything else in the figure should be derived from it visibly.

When a visualization feels like it is asserting a result rather than showing why it holds, suspect you are looking at a projection. Ask what got projected away.

You“When showing the worst-case path, maybe it will touch the corner of this arc in a way we can understand?”

Exactly, and provably. The worst-case tip at step j is placed at radius rad(j) and angle ±arccos(shad(j)/rad(j)) — those are the coordinates of the corner where chord meets arc. Verified to 3×10−15 over 615 tips. The zigzag is the sequence of cap corners, and since there are two corners, it alternates.

The check also exposed a defect. wcTip used the clamped cone angle, which is 180° while the shadow is negative — putting the path at radius rad(j) in a direction with w*·w = −rad(j), below the shadow floor. The extremal run was being drawn outside the region the proof allows. Corrected, its step-0 corner is w₀ itself.

Predict first, then check

“Is there anything interesting here?” gets you a tour. “I predict the path touches the corner” gets you a yes or no, and when the answer is nearly-but-not-quite, it gets you the reason.

The near-miss is the prize. A prediction that is exactly right confirms what you already believed. A prediction that is 95% right is pointing at a bug — here, a worst case drawn outside the feasible region, which nobody would have found by looking.

Say the guess out loud before asking the model to test it, and commit to it. Then insist on a numerical tolerance over many instances, so “yes” means something.

You“Why wouldn’t the spray be such that all the blue rays go on one side and all the white rays on the other? I guess that won’t happen unless we already apply the separating plane rule to know which direction to draw the ray, right?”

The colours interleave because the ray is y·x — the label is already folded in, and that multiplication is exactly what maps both classes into one fan. Drawing the raw x instead would separate them, and needs no knowledge of w*, only the labels.

But the instinct was onto something one step over: the fact that the y·x fan fits inside a half-plane at all is linear separability. Measured: all 640 rays within 84° of w*.

You“Switch to raw x.”

Done, with the cost named in the same breath: for a − point the update now runs along the mirror ray, so the red arrow lies on its own ray only half the time.

You“Now that you’ve drawn it, I see. Mathematically the right thing in weight space is the dual hyperplanes — the line orthogonal to each point. Let’s go back to the dual lines, coloured by class.”

Back to {w : w·x = 0} per point, full lines, in class colours. This also restores the wedge’s construction: it is the overlap of the half-planes these bound, and its two edges lie along two of the drawn lines — confirmed to 1.2×10−3, which is the step size of the search that finds them.

Build the wrong one and look at it

This is the centre of the whole transcript, and it is a detour. Three versions in a row, ending back near where it started but with the person now understanding why.

Notice the direction of travel: the student corrected the model, not the reverse — and only after seeing the thing rendered. No amount of argument would have got there. Twenty minutes of discussion lost to three minutes of rendering.

The precondition is reversibility. Every step here was one commit, so a wrong turn cost nothing. If your changes are cheap to undo, looking is almost always faster than reasoning about what you would see.

Do not let the model talk you out of building something you want to look at, and do not let it silently build it either. The safe version of compliance is the one that names the cost while it complies. If a substantive change arrives with no downside attached, ask: “what does this version make worse?”

The same confusion that caused that detour also produced a clean result. w has to sit within 90° of every ray, so within 90° of both extremes; and if those two are S apart, what satisfies both is an interval of width 180° − S. So:

fan span + wedge width = 180° — measured 179.87 to 179.98 across 20 datasets, the residual being the 0.0012 rad step of the search. A tight fan leaves a wide wedge; a fan spreading toward 180° squeezes the wedge to nothing, which is separability failing.

Confusion is a lead, not an embarrassment

The question that started this was, on its face, a misunderstanding — an expectation that the colours would separate, which was wrong about the figure as drawn.

Chasing it anyway produced a redrawn figure, a correction to the geometry, and an exact identity. The misunderstanding was pointing at real structure the whole time.

When something looks like it ought to be true and is not, ask why before assuming the answer is boring. Phrase it as a claim you expect to be wrong, so the model has something specific to refute.

You“When we choose a mistake, should we put a green dot in the right place in weight space? It would show up on the corresponding spray line.”

The model picked one of several readings, built an elaborate crossing-point marker, measured its distribution across 191 updates, found a genuine step-dependent pattern, and wrote it all up.

You“Maybe you are imagining a different dot than I am?”

Yes. Three candidate dots existed — the tip of y·x, the tip of w+y·x, and the crossing on the line — and the request did not distinguish them. Only after enumerating all three did the real answer appear, which was a fourth thing: at that stage a point had no position in weight space at all, because its dual is a line.

Where the model wasted the round

It did not hallucinate. It did something more ordinary and harder to catch: it resolved an ambiguity silently, in favour of whichever reading it could build.

Models are tuned to produce an artifact. Given three readings of a request, the strong default is to pick one and start working, and the resulting work looks committed and well-verified — which makes it harder, not easier, to notice the reading was never agreed.

For anything geometric or ambiguous, put the enumeration before the construction: “list the candidate objects this could mean, say which satisfy my constraint, and wait.” One sentence, one round saved.

You“Maybe label it pink: Cauchy–Schwarz limit, or pink: bounded region for worst-case w?”

Neither, and the reason matters. The region is the raw conjunction of the two inductive bounds; Cauchy–Schwarz is the step you apply to it to extract the cone, so that name attaches to the input rather than the output. And it is not the worst-case region either: it holds every w still permitted, of which the worst case is only the corner. The label became “where w must lie after k mistakes” — necessary, not sufficient.

The signal you want from a model

Both suggestions were plausible, both were offered by the person steering, and both were wrong in a way that would have quietly taught something false to a room full of students.

A model that accepts your terminology is worth very little. Naming is where misconception hides, because a wrong name is repeatable, memorable, and invisible in the figure itself.

Propose your own names deliberately and ask the model to argue against them. If it never pushes back on a name, treat that as a broken instrument rather than as agreement.

What to actually do

The eight moves, in the order they tend to come up

  1. Demand a distribution, not a confirmation. Name a count, a tolerance and a number of trials. “Over 200 picks, how often was it the worst, and how many distinct ones were there?” The degenerate case cannot survive a table.
  2. Trade benchmarks for derivations, in both directions. Given a number, ask why from the theorem. Given an argument, ask for the measurement. A model that can produce only one of the two is telling you something about how well it understands — and about how well you do.
  3. Re-read every word on the figure after every change. Stale labels mark the exact place where the meaning moved while nobody was looking.
  4. Ask what set the theorem constrains, and insist that set is what gets drawn. A picture of a consequence looks right and teaches less.
  5. Predict out loud before checking. Then chase the near-misses, which is where the defects live. Two real bugs in this transcript were found that way and no other.
  6. When torn between two designs, build the cheap one and look. This requires that changes are cheap to revert, which is a reason to work in small commits rather than a matter of taste.
  7. Ask what each change makes worse. A substantive change offered with no downside attached is a change whose cost nobody has worked out.
  8. Enumerate before constructing. When a request could mean three things, say so and ask for the list first. Models resolve ambiguity silently and then verify the wrong thing thoroughly.

On the model agreeing with you

The dangerous moments in this transcript were not the ones where the model was wrong. They were the ones where it was accommodating: building the raw-x spray on request, picking one reading of an ambiguous dot and verifying it carefully, accepting a framing and running with it.

None of those look like errors while they are happening. They look like progress. What made them survivable was that each change was reversible, each claim came with a number attached, and the figure was looked at again afterwards.

So the test to apply is not “is the model lying to me?” It is: when did it last tell me I was wrong? In this conversation the model refused two proposed names, contradicted the premise of a question about the spray, and reported that its own earlier drawing placed the worst-case path outside the feasible region. If a long session contains none of that, the problem is not that the model has been correct throughout.

What the student ends up with

Not a proof they can recite. A picture with a mechanism in it: a chord that marches outward linearly while an arc grows as the square root, a corner where the two meet that is the only point saturating both bounds at once, and a wedge whose width is what is left of 180° after the data has had its say.

Every one of those came from a question that started in confusion. The finished demo shows none of the confusion, which is precisely why the finished demo is not the thing worth studying.
