Interrogating a proof with an LLM — an annotated transcript

A record of building the perceptron convergence demo across two conversations with an LLM agent: one in a plain chat that produced the first working figure, and one in a coding agent that rebuilt most of what that figure meant. Almost nothing here was specified in advance, and the questions are the part worth keeping.

The centre column has the prompts, what came back, and the figure as it stood at that moment. The margin has the reasoning that led to the next question.

Three things working against you

You do not understand it yet. That is the situation you are trying to get out of, so you cannot specify what you want up front. You can only react to what you are shown.

The model will build what you asked for. Including when what you asked for is wrong. It is much better at satisfying a request than at telling you the request was malformed.

You can both be wrong at once. The failure that matters is not the model hallucinating a fact. It is the two of you settling on a picture that looks right, reads well, and quietly misrepresents the theorem. Nothing in the conversation will flag this. Only a check will.

Everything below is a defence against one of those three.

Part one — getting the first picture

This half ran in a plain chat, starting from “I’m going to teach Rosenblatt’s algorithm today, what is the best way to teach it?” Interactive artifacts are not kept in shared conversations, so the first two figures here are reconstructions, redrawn from screenshots; the rest were rebuilt by running the original code for each step.

You“Simplify the proof for me; make it common-sense for whiteboard argument.”

“Whiteboard version: two gauges on the board, one counting real progress, one counting bloat. Progress climbs steadily. Bloat can't keep up. When they cross, you're out of mistakes.”

Then the units, set first so the rest reads in one scale: the longest input has length 1, and γ is how far the good separator keeps points off the fence. Gauge 1 is the shadow on true north, climbing by at least γ a mistake. Gauge 2 is the length, which cannot keep up because the correction always leans backward.

That vocabulary — gauges, true north, progress against bloat — is what the rest of both conversations ran on.

Asking for it in plain words, before asking for a picture

This came second, before any figure existed. What it produced was not a summary of the algebra but a different handle on it, in words that could be said out loud at a board.

A sentence that resists being put plainly is usually covering for something not yet pinned down, so the request doubles as a check on whether there is a claim underneath the notation.

It also set a trap, which the next beat walks into: the plain framing was two quantities crossing, and the first figure drawn was a literal picture of that framing.

Ask for the common-sense version early, and separately from any request to draw. They are different questions, and the answer to the first is not a specification for the second.

You“Please draw the diagram here for me. Maybe with js/svg?”

What came back was a plot of the two sides of the inequality: kγ rising as a straight line, √k as a curve, a slider for γ, and the crossing point running away as the margin shrinks.

Then a second figure, for why the length barely grows: the correction leans backward against w at more than 90°, so the triangle closes short of the Pythagorean case.

You“The plot is not informative, but I think that the triangles could be. But you’ve plotted w without plotting w* and you are not showing the mass of other points. I think to explain it would be helpful to show the points.”

“Fair.” Redrawn with the data, the true separator, its margin corridor, and w’s boundary rotating as you step through mistakes.

Drawing the algebra instead of the situation

Asked for the diagram, the model plotted the last line of the proof: two functions of k, crossing. It is a correct picture of the inequality and it contains no data, no w, and no w*.

That is a natural reading of “the diagram” when the preceding text was algebra. It is also a picture of the symbols rather than of what the symbols describe.

The triangle got partial credit at the time — the triangles could be informative — and the verdict since is that neither figure earned its place. It abstracts the vectors away from any data just as the first abstracts the quantities into functions of k. Neither contains a point, a w*, or the corridor.

Check whether the figure contains the objects the theorem is about, or only quantities derived from them. If the data is not in it, say which parts of the setup you want drawn.

You“I like the margin figure, but the ringed point you’ve drawn is not on the wrong side. We need some examples that are on the wrong side!”

“Caught it: I was ringing the point and applying the update in the same click, so you only ever saw the boundary that had already fixed it.” Split into two clicks — find the violation, look at it, then apply.

Running that build also shows the second defect, the one that surfaces in the next exchange: after a single update 0 of its 14 points are misclassified, so a second click has nothing to find.

That split is why the finished demo has a Find mistake step at all.

The figure contradicting its own caption

The label said the ringed point was misclassified. The picture showed it on the correct side. Both were rendered from the same state, so one of them was wrong.

The cause was timing rather than mathematics: the ring and the update landed in the same frame.

When a figure asserts something about a specific element, look at that element and check the assertion by eye. Bugs in what is shown do not appear in the code that computes it.

You“I liked the previous visualization better. Except that find a mistake didn’t work! I want a 2d scatterplot of data points where the initial model misclassifies many of them.”

“The dataset was the problem: after one update w already separated everything, so the second click had nothing to find.” Rebuilt starting from a badly wrong w, 12 of 16 points misclassified at the outset, every wrong point carrying a red halo.

That became the default the demo is still built around, and the same request reappears much later as “start w within 15° of −w*”.

The reply also said the halo count “does not fall monotonically” and that somewhere in the middle a correction fixes one point and breaks two. Running the whole sequence, the count goes 12, 12, 12, 6, 2, 0 and never rises once. True of the algorithm, not true of this figure.

An instance too easy to show anything

Nothing was broken. The figure was correct, the algorithm was correct, and the example converged before the thing being demonstrated could happen.

Choosing the instance is part of building the visualization, not a detail underneath it.

Ask what the hardest honest case looks like, and make that the default. A demo that succeeds immediately has nothing left to show.

You“It’s great, except that it doesn’t show how w is updated from the error case. Is it a parallelogram?”

Yes, and the second side is the data point itself: the correction y·x is the point’s own position vector drawn from the origin, flipped through the origin when the label is negative. So w and the point are two sides and the new w is the diagonal.

Which makes visible something the pseudocode hides: the correction has no free parameters. No gradient, no step size, no loss being differentiated.

Guessing, then asking

The question carried a specific guess. That is cheaper to answer than an open request, and the answer comes back sharper because there is something definite to confirm or deny.

When you half-see a structure, name it in the question. “Is it a parallelogram” gets a better answer than “how should the update be drawn”.

You“You haven’t highlighted the narrowing solution space, but I’d like that.”

Added as a violet wedge that starts unbounded and is sliced by each constraint the run bumps into, closing onto the green solution wedge. Presented alongside the convergence discussion, with several paragraphs on what it shows.

You“The cone on the right that you said is used to prove convergence doesn’t really make sense to me — e.g. the w isn’t in that blueish cone — what’s it bounding? Are we drawing the wrong vector?”

“You’re right, and the fault is mine: that violet wedge bounds the solutions, not w. w is never promised to be inside it. It’s bookkeeping about which constraints have been ruled out, and it has no role in the convergence proof. I put it next to the proof talk and made it look load-bearing.”

The region that does bound w is a different one: centred on w*, half-angle arccos(√k·γ/R). That is the ancestor of the pink region in the finished demo, and it exists because of this question.

Is the thing the theorem talks about inside the region?

The wedge had been requested, built to specification, and then described as the convergence argument. Nothing in the exchange flagged it. It was correct geometry doing the wrong job.

What separated them was a question with a yes or no answer: the theorem is about w, so is w in there? It was not, and it was never going to be.

For each region in a figure, name the object it constrains and check that object is inside it on screen. “Nothing, it is context” is a fine answer — what is worth catching is a region sitting next to the proof and borrowing its authority.

You“The blue cone is nice now, but the example you’ve shown has an initialization that is too favourable and doesn’t iterate enough. Give me a more prototypical example with more iterations.”

Margin narrowed to about 0.19 of the data radius, with two large-norm points near the corridor edges that keep knocking w past each other. The bound goes from 7 mistakes to 28, and the run takes a while.

Tuning the instance until the bound bites

The previous data was separable with room to spare, so the run ended long before the cone closed and the squeeze the proof describes never appeared on screen.

The fix is a deliberate choice of data: push two points up against the corridor edges, which drops γ without touching R and stretches the run.

When a demonstration finishes too easily, the knob is usually in the instance rather than the code. Ask which quantity in the bound controls the difficulty, then set it explicitly.

You“To explain the bounds cone, I think we should show a hypothetical worst-case rollout of the worst possible zig-zag of updates, starting from the worst case and updating up to step t, showing how it stays in the cone.”

The extremal walk is pinned: every step must add exactly γ of shadow and exactly R² of length-squared, so the tip sits at kγ horizontally and √k·R in radius, riding the cone edge at every k. Three conditions, each the extreme of one inequality in the proof.

With a caveat offered unprompted: staying perpendicular to w at every step needs a fresh direction each time, so this trajectory requires dimension at least k. In two dimensions it dies after a couple of steps, which is why the real runs sit well inside the region rather than on its edge.

You“How hard is it to construct data points and an unlucky ordering that actually exercises the worst case?”

“Trivially easy in high dimensions, and apparently impossible in two. The lever is dimension, not ordering.” Then the construction: the standard basis in Rn, all labelled +1, gives exactly n mistakes against a bound of n.

And an admission: “What the worst case actually is in fixed small d, I don’t know off the top of my head, and I’d want to check rather than guess.”

Asking how tight the bound really is

The question was about the gap between the drawn region and what the algorithm does. The answer named a real limitation of the figure: in two dimensions the extremal walk cannot be realised, so the pink path is a bound and not something the demo could ever produce.

The closing “I don’t know” is the useful kind of answer. An estimate would have been easy to produce and hard to check.

Ask what the figure is promising that the situation cannot deliver. A model willing to say a construction is unreachable in the case you are drawing is giving you more than one that explains the construction well.

You“On the current data shown, why is it that after the second iteration the w vector is not in the blue cone? Is the blue cone more aggressive than the real bound?”

“Good catch, and the answer is yes.” Both gauges assume w starts at zero. With a nonzero start they pick up offsets, so the honest bound is cosθk ≥ (w*·w₀ + kγ) / √(|w₀|² + kR²). The default start pointed away from w*, making the numerator negative for the first few steps, where the true region is the whole plane.

You“Certainly when k=0 the blue cone should be the full 360, right?”

Right: with no mistakes yet, nothing has been paid off and the bound constrains nothing. The slider had not gone down to 0, and the vacuous case is now drawn as a full disc.

The same check again, further on

The identical question — is w inside it — came back a second time, against the standalone build, and caught an unrelated defect: a formula valid only for w₀ = 0 being drawn over a run that starts elsewhere.

Then the boundary case. At k = 0 the theorem says nothing, and a figure that draws a tight region there is claiming more than the theorem does.

Keep one containment check and re-run it after every change. Check the ends too: what should the figure show where the statement is vacuous, and does it.

How this went in practice

The starting point was not knowing what the figure should show. That is the reason for doing it, so the specification arrived late and in pieces, assembled out of reactions to things already on screen.

The model built what it was asked for. Requests that turned out to be malformed still produced working code, and the results looked finished.

A figure can look correct and still show something other than what the theorem says. That does not come up in conversation. It comes up when someone checks a number or reads a label a second time.

The margin notes are mostly about those three situations.

Part two — rebuilding what it meant

The first conversation ended by handing the page to a coding agent with repository access. From here the figures are recoverable, pulled from the commits themselves.

What part one produced

A working demo of the perceptron mistake bound. It ran, it was correct, and it looked finished. Most of its elements changed meaning over the rounds that follow.

You“When it finds a mistake, don’t take the first one it happens to scan. Sort the mistakes by which produces the worst updated w — the maximum angle with w* — and pick among the worst two.”

Implemented, then measured rather than asserted: over 197 picks the chosen update was the worst available every time. The random choice between two never varied.

Each point sat in the array twice, as +x and as −x labelled minus, and both produce the same update y·x. The two worst were one update counted twice. Deduplicating by update vector made the choice real: 98/68 across the worst and second-worst.

Asking for a number instead of a yes

“Did it work” returns yes.

What surfaced the duplication was asking for a distribution: over 200 picks, how often was the choice the worst available, and how many distinct updates existed.

Name a count, a tolerance and a number of trials, and read the result before treating a behavioural change as done.

You“Make the positive and negative cases unsymmetric when generating the points. And pick the initial w within 15° of −w*.”

This is where the duplication came from. The generator emitted points in antipodal pairs, so the cloud was point-symmetric and the two halves of each pair shared one update. Drawing each point independently removes it at the source.

Starting w near −w* begins the run in the hardest honest state. Across 20 datasets: initial angle 166°–179°, label splits varying (17/15, 20/12, 14/18), no surviving symmetric pairs, no run over its own bound.

Where the duplicates came from

The earlier check found two candidate updates that were identical. Deduplicating handled the symptom.

The cause was upstream, in how the data was made. Point-symmetric data is a special case, and the demo had been running on it.

When a check turns up a degeneracy, trace it to where it was created. Then make the hardest honest configuration the default, so the bound is exercised rather than assumed.

You“Is worst angle to w* the right heuristic, or should we be sorting by worst dot product?”

Answered with a benchmark: over 40 datasets replayed from identical starts, angle-ordering forced more mistakes (mean 19.3 vs 17.1, winning 27 of 40). The two orders disagreed on 56% of steps.

You“You can reason about it from the structure of the proof.”

The bound constrains a ratio, cosθ = (w*·w)/|w|. Since w*·w is common to every candidate, dot-product ordering reduces to argmin over margins, which tightens the numerator and leaves the denominator slack. Angle ordering targets the quantity being bounded. There is also a sign flip: while the shadow is negative the adversary wants |w| small, and once it is positive, large. Dot-ordering never sees the denominator, so it cannot follow that.

Asking for the derivation as well as the benchmark

The first answer was a measurement. It says which option won without saying why, and the why is what carries to the next question.

One follow-up produced the reason. If the derivation does not come, that is information too: there is a correlation available and not yet a model of it.

Ask both directions. Given a number, ask for the argument. Given an argument, ask for the number. Both mattered here — the argument explained the ranking, the measurement showed the two orders disagree often enough for the choice to matter.

You“The worst-case cone should have another colour so we can talk about it out loud in class unambiguously. Yellow or pink?”

Pink. Amber was already w and its separating plane, which sit inside the cone in the same panel, so yellow would have produced a yellow arrow inside a yellow cone.

You“Why does it say cone: every direction?”

That label was written when pink meant only the cone. By this point it announced that nothing was constrained, printed across a visibly bounded region.

Reading the labels again after a change

The figure and its caption are separate claims, and they had drifted apart across versions.

Asking what to call something out loud in class is a practical way to catch it. A name that will not survive being said in a room usually belongs to something not yet pinned down.

After each change to the figure, read every word still on it.

You“Would it be informative for the pink cone to also show the bounds for the length of w?”

This one changed the figure most. What the two inequalities pin down is a circular cap: the disc of radius √(|w₀|²+kR²) cut by the chord at w*·w₀+kγ. The cone was its angular hull, which is a projection of that. Drawing the cap shows the mechanism the cone states: the chord moves outward linearly in k while the arc grows as √k, and the cone closes when they meet.

What the proof constrains, and what the picture drew

The cone followed from the two inequalities rather than being what they pin down. A picture of a consequence can be entirely correct and still carry less.

The question that moved this along: what exactly is the set of w the proof still permits after k mistakes. Then draw that set and derive the rest from it.

If a figure seems to state a result rather than show how it comes about, ask what has been projected away.

You“When showing the worst-case path, maybe it will touch the corner of this arc in a way we can understand?”

It does. The worst-case tip at step j sits at radius rad(j) and angle ±arccos(shad(j)/rad(j)), which are the coordinates of the corner where chord meets arc — checked to 3×10−15 over 615 tips. There are two corners, which is why it alternates.

The check also turned up a defect. wcTip had been using the clamped cone angle, 180° while the shadow is negative, which placed the tip at radius rad(j) in a direction with w*·w = −rad(j) — below the shadow floor. The extremal run was being drawn outside the region the proof allows. Corrected, its step-0 corner is w₀.

Saying the prediction first

Asking whether anything interesting is there produces a survey. Stating the guess produces a yes or a no.

This guess was nearly right, and the gap held a bug: a worst-case path drawn outside the feasible region, which reading the figure would not have caught.

State the guess before the check, and ask for a tolerance over many instances so the answer carries information.

You“Why wouldn’t the spray be such that all the blue rays go on one side and all the white rays on the other? I guess that won’t happen unless we already apply the separating plane rule to know which direction to draw the ray, right?”

The colours interleave because the ray is y·x, so the label is already folded in; that multiplication is what maps both classes into one fan. Drawing raw x separates them, and needs only the labels.

The premise was close to something else, though: the y·x fan fitting inside a half-plane is linear separability. All 640 rays measured within 84° of w*.

You“Switch to raw x.”

Done, with the cost stated: for a − point the update runs along the mirror ray, so the red arrow lies on its own ray only half the time.

You“Now that you’ve drawn it, I see. Mathematically the right thing in weight space is the dual hyperplanes — the line orthogonal to each point. Let’s go back to the dual lines, coloured by class.”

Back to {w : w·x = 0} per point, full lines, in class colours. That also brings back the wedge’s construction: it is the overlap of the half-planes these bound, and its two edges lie along two of the drawn lines, to 1.2×10−3, which is the step size of the search that locates them.

Building it to look at it

Three versions, ending near where it started, with the understanding arriving in between.

The correction came from the person, after seeing it rendered. The discussion beforehand had not produced it.

Each step was one commit, so the detour cost a few minutes. That is what made rendering cheaper than reasoning about what the rendering would look like.

When two designs are hard to choose between and both are cheap, build both. Asking what a change makes worse helps: here the answer was that the arrow would lie on its ray only half the time, which is the detail that decided it later.

The same confusion produced a result. w has to sit within 90° of every ray, so within 90° of both extremes; if those are S apart, what satisfies both is an interval of width 180° − S. So fan span + wedge width = 180°, measured 179.87 to 179.98 across 20 datasets, the residual being the 0.0012 rad step of the search.

A tight fan leaves a wide wedge. A fan spreading toward 180° leaves none, which is separability failing.

Following the confusion

The question came from expecting something that was not true of the figure as drawn.

Following it anyway produced a redrawn figure, a correction to the geometry, and this identity.

Ask about things that seem like they ought to be true. Phrasing them as claims gives the model something specific to refute.

You“When we choose a mistake, should we put a green dot in the right place in weight space? It would show up on the corresponding spray line.”

The model picked one reading, built a crossing-point marker, measured its distribution over 191 updates, found a step-dependent pattern in it, and wrote it up.

You“Maybe you are imagining a different dot than I am?”

Three candidates existed — the tip of y·x, the tip of w+y·x, and the crossing on the line — and the request did not separate them. Listing all three led to a fourth answer: at that stage a point has no position in weight space, because its dual is a line.

Three readings of one request

The request had at least three referents. One was chosen and built, with measurements attached.

The work was careful, which made the mismatch harder to spot than a straightforward error would have been.

For ambiguous geometry, ask for the list before the implementation: name the candidates, say which satisfy the constraint, and wait.

You“Maybe label it pink: Cauchy–Schwarz limit, or pink: bounded region for worst-case w?”

Neither fits. The region is the conjunction of the two inductive bounds; Cauchy–Schwarz is the step applied to it to get the cone, so that name belongs to the input. And it holds every w still permitted, not the worst one, which is its corner. The label became “where w must lie after k mistakes” — necessary rather than sufficient.

Naming things

Both suggestions were reasonable and neither described the region.

Naming is where a misconception can sit for a long time, because a name repeats and the figure does not contradict it.

Propose names deliberately and ask for arguments against them.

The strategy underneath

What the questions were drawn from

The statement of the theorem, held next to the picture. Nearly every question that changed something traces back to a specific clause. “What’s it bounding?” comes from knowing the theorem bounds w. “Reason from the structure of the proof” comes from knowing the conclusion is about a ratio. The figure gets compared against the sentence, not against taste.

Shape sense that runs ahead of the algebra. “Is it a parallelogram?” “Maybe it touches the corner.” “Shouldn’t those be half-lines?” None of these were verified before being asked. Half-seeing a structure and naming it is enough, and the naming is what makes the answer checkable.

The room. “So we can talk about it out loud in class unambiguously.” Imagining saying a thing to thirty people is a constraint the mathematics does not supply, and it caught both a stale label and two wrong names.

The ends of the range. What should this show at k = 0, where the theorem says nothing? What happens when the margin goes to zero? Degenerate cases are cheap to ask about and the answers are unambiguous.

Looking. Several turns are just the rendering arriving and the understanding changing: “now that you’ve drawn it, I see.” That is a use of the model as a rendering surface rather than as a source of answers, and it is the fastest thing in either transcript.

Where to press, and where to let it run

The pressure is not applied evenly. Layout, colour and wording were handed over with almost no argument — say what you want, look at the result, adjust. What got interrogated was every claim about what a drawn thing means.

The sharpest moment to press is when the model narrates significance. The violet-wedge problem happened exactly where the reply said, in effect, here is the convergence argument made visible. Adjacency to a proof is not a role in it, and a figure placed beside an argument will be read as part of it.

Openness is cheapest where building is cheap. “Switch to raw x” cost three minutes and settled a question that discussion had not. When a change is one commit, rendering beats reasoning about what the rendering would look like.

The model is also worth using as a source of structure and not only as a draftsman. Kaczmarz, the standard-basis construction that saturates the bound, the dimension argument for why two dimensions cannot realise the worst case — none of those were prompted by someone who already knew them. Take the mathematics, then check it.

What agreement looks like from the inside

Across both conversations the model opened replies with “Fair”, “Good catch”, “Good instinct”, “You’re right, and the fault is mine.” Those phrases attach equally to the turns that fixed something real and the turns that built the wrong thing well. The warmth carries no information.

The failure mode here was never flattery. It was unearned significance: a correct object given a role it did not have. The model’s own description afterwards is exact — I put it next to the proof talk and made it look load-bearing. Nothing in the conversation could have surfaced that, because the conversation was about what the wedge showed, and it did show that.

What surfaced it was leaving the conversation and looking at the screen with the theorem in mind. The check is mechanical: for each element, what object does it constrain, and is that object where the element says it should be. Run it after every change. It caught two separate defects in the same sitting, and both times the answer was available in one glance once the question was asked.

The signals worth noticing run the other way. The model refused two proposed names, contradicted the premise of a question about the spray, volunteered that the extremal path needs dimension at least k and so cannot occur in the figure being drawn, and said plainly that it did not know the worst case in fixed small dimension and would want to check. Those turns moved the understanding more than any of the agreements.

The shape of it

No part of this was specified in advance. The sequence is: ask for something, look at what arrives, find the one element that does not fit, ask what it means, and trace the answer to its cause rather than patching where it showed.

Two conversations and about forty rounds produced a figure whose parts each do a job: a chord moving outward linearly while an arc grows as the square root, the corner where they meet, the only point saturating both bounds at once, and a wedge whose width is what remains of 180° once the data has had its say.

Each of those came from a question that started in confusion, and the finished demo shows none of it.
